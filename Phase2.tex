\section*{B. Public Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{B. Public Summary}
\noindent\textit{Word count: \Bwordcount / 150}\par\medskip


\vspace{1em}
\noindent Our Environmental Sustainability project introduces Eco-log, an AI-backed web tool designed to help university researchers check how their work connects to already existing studies. It fixes a major problem: how can laboratories avoid repeating expensive and resource-heavy experiments? By generating two interactive visual trees, this tool highlights hidden research gaps as well as local opportunities to reuse data and laboratory equipment.
This tool is very important for the TU/e community and the public because it turns high-level campus climate goals into practical, daily actions. Instead of buying new materials or doing the same tests over again, researchers can easily find nearby collaborators to share physical equipments or digital datasets.

Our major anticipated challenges include: (i) keeping campus resource records updated. (ii) Helping users see the difference between a cool new idea and its actual impact on the world. (iii) Preventing researchers from blindly trusting automated AI suggestions.

\section*{C. Comprehensive Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{C. Comprehensive Summary}
\noindent\textit{Word count: \Cwordcount / 2000}\par\medskip

\subsection*{Problem Definition and Objective(s)}

A student starting a new project may find a paper on the same topic yet overlook a useful method or piece of equipment in another TU/e department. The university's research portal already lists projects, publications and equipment \citep{tueportal}. Our project addresses the difficulty of finding relevant work before the student knows the right title, author or search term. The extent of this problem at TU/e has not yet been established and would need to be investigated with researchers.

We propose a website where a student or researcher enters a short description of a planned study: the question, method and, if relevant, equipment needed. The site would show related TU/e work and explain the connection. For example, someone planning a LiDAR (light detection and ranging) experiment might find a similar sensing method used for a different purpose in another department. They could then ask whether a protocol, dataset or setup can be reused. This may save time and resources, but finding a match does not itself save electricity or materials. We would test whether people actually change their plans after using the site.

The main users would be students and researchers at an early planning stage. Project leaders might use it to find partners. Librarians, equipment managers and data stewards would help keep records useful and decide what can be shared. Researchers whose projects appear in the results should be able to decide whether details of unfinished work are visible. The tool would support their judgment; it would not decide whether a project is original or deserves funding. Contacting another group can also create work for that group. The site should help users decide when a conversation is worthwhile rather than send automatic requests to everyone with a similar project.

\subsection*{Background of Current Approaches and Knowledge}

The TU/e research portal provides the starting point. We would build on records that the university allows us to reuse rather than create a second collection from scratch. Public indexes such as OpenAlex could give some wider context for published work \citep{openalex2026}. Neither source, however, tells us about every ongoing project or confidential idea. For an initial version, we would use permitted titles, abstracts and equipment descriptions. A researcher could also choose to add a short summary of work in progress. Every result would show where it came from and when it was last updated.

Existing research gives us a reason to look for connections, but it does not tell us exactly what TU/e researchers need. In one study at a US university, later collaboration was most common when researchers had some topic overlap, rather than almost none or almost complete overlap \citep{smith2021}. A study including TU/e also argues that sustainability work depends on cooperation across departments and on support from the university \citep{karo2026}. These findings suggest possible benefits, yet we still need to ask local users when they welcome a match and when it might feel like competition or unwanted exposure.

Scientific-text representation methods provide a technical approach to explore. Models such as SPECTER compare scientific texts using titles, abstracts and citation links \citep{cohan2020}. Later work shows that performance differs between search and ranking tasks \citep{singh2023}. We therefore cannot assume that an embedding model, which represents text numerically, will reliably match short project plans. We would compare it with ordinary keyword search and ask researchers whether the suggested links make sense. We would record our search terms, sources and selection decisions so that the background review can be checked. For example, a sensing method might appear in robotics and building research under different keywords. A method-based link could reveal this, though a researcher still has to decide whether the technique really transfers.

\subsection*{Plan of Approach / Design Strategy}

We would use Value Sensitive Design to examine whose needs the site serves and how its features affect stakeholders \citep{friedman2008}. The initial values are sustainability, privacy, fairness, researchers' control over their ideas and explicability. Explicability requires users to understand why projects are linked. Privacy matters because even a brief description may reveal an unpublished research direction. Fairness matters because departments with more complete records may appear more often in results. These values provide a starting point for stakeholder discussion rather than a final list defined on users' behalf.

First, we would speak with researchers from different departments, a librarian or research-support worker, and a data steward. We would ask how they currently find related work, what makes a match useful, and which details they would be willing to share. A simple mock-up would help them react to something concrete. Their comments would shape the required fields, sharing options and explanations. TU/e requires ethical review for research with participants or personal data, so any interviews or user tests would follow the relevant procedure \citep{tueethics}.

The initial prototype would request a short description of the problem, method and possible application. It would search permitted records by keywords and by scientific-text similarity. Users could indicate whether topic, method or equipment is their priority. Results would be grouped under these headings, with a link back to the original record. We would avoid presenting one exact similarity percentage, since the same number could hide different kinds of connection. If an abstract is missing or a department is poorly covered, the site should say so. We would use a compact search method first and add generated wording only if users understand the results better without extra errors or unreasonable computing costs.

The proposed interface has three linked trees. A \emph{similarity tree} shows which records share a topic, method or resource with the user's idea. A \emph{difference tree} points out how the idea differs from the records found; it cannot prove that the difference is a new research gap. A \emph{future tree} gives questions the researcher could discuss with a supervisor, based on the same records. Each branch should lead to its source. Users could mark a bad match, save a useful one or ask for contact through an approved channel. The tree design is meant to make the information easier to explore; a tree image alone is not evidence of sustainability.

A user's draft description would not automatically join the shared database. They could separately agree to share a short summary, choose who may see it and later withdraw it. TU/e staff would have to approve storage, access and maintenance arrangements under the university's research-data policy \citep{tuerdm}. Maintenance would also require responsibility for correcting outdated records and handling complaints about incorrect links. Without this work, even a good search model would soon become less useful.

We would begin with a small set of records from two departments and review missed matches with researchers before expanding it. That would let us check permissions and record quality without assuming that one method works equally well across the university. We would then test the prototype with tasks drawn from real project-planning situations. Users would try both the current portal and the new interface. We would ask domain experts to judge whether the top matches are relevant and whether the explanations are accurate. We would measure time to find a useful record or contact and note where users correct or reject a suggestion. Later, we would ask whether a match led to a conversation, reuse of data or equipment, or a changed plan. These outcomes are more directly connected to the project objective than click counts. Any estimate of saved resources would need a clear comparison case and would include the site's own computing costs. This is the path by which the tool might help environmental sustainability, but its effect remains to be shown.

\subsection*{Comparison to Alternatives}

Our Phase 1 idea was an AI-supported self-assessment of a research activity's environmental footprint. That could be useful once a workflow is defined, but it needs measurements and reference data. The new idea acts earlier, while the researcher can still choose a method or reuse an existing resource. A simpler option would be to improve the current portal's search filters without AI. We would keep that as the baseline; if semantic matching does not help users find better results, the simpler option is preferable.

The main challenges are incomplete records, different terms across fields, confidential work and misleading matches. A polished visualisation could make a weak match appear more certain than the evidence supports. We would show sources and missing information and let researchers correct results. We also need to avoid implying that similar work makes a new project pointless: a similar method can serve a different question. The aim for this phase is a design that can be tested honestly, not a claim that the website has already reduced research waste.

\section*{D. Responsible Innovation Project Statement}
\noindent\textit{Word count: \Dwordcount / 1000}\par\medskip
This statement examines transparency, bias and reliability in the proposed research-matching tool. The measures below address the values identified in our Value Sensitive Design approach: explicability, fairness and researchers' control over results \citep{friedman2008}.

\subsection*{Transparency}
Users submit a research description and receive potentially relevant previous work. They need to understand why each source was selected and be able to check the AI's explanation. We propose the following measures:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Source transparency:} Each source should include its title, authors, publication year and a link to the original source, with additional information where available. Researchers can then verify the sources rather than rely solely on the AI.
    \item \textbf{Explanation of relevance:} Each source should have a short explanation of why it was selected, identifying similarities in research questions, methods or other relevant aspects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Bias and coverage}
The tool's results may be biased by the sources available to it. For example, access limited to journal articles or English-language sources could cause researchers to miss relevant previous work. We propose the following measures to reduce this risk:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Database coverage:} Users should be able to see which databases the tool searches, so that they understand its limitations.
    \item \textbf{Search strategies:} Where possible, the search should use synonyms, similarities across disciplines and different terminology.
    \item \textbf{User input:} Users should be able to adjust their search query, giving them more control when the AI encounters limitations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Reliability}
AI is not always 100\% reliable. The search results from the tool can contain errors. If the user does not check the results, it may happen that they make a wrong conclusion. For example research could be different in reality but according to the tool very similar. To prevent this the following measures should be taken:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Access to original:} The user should be able to inspect the papers from the results themselves. By doing this they can check whether the results are relevant to the research. 
    
    \item \textbf{Limitations:} The limitations of the tool should be made clear to the user. If the information the tool obtains is limited it should indicate the low condifence in the studies being similar. Additionally, no relevant results from the tool does not automatically mean that no previous research exists on the topic.
    
    \item \textbf{Human verification:} The user should still confirm themselves whether a source provided is relevant or not. The tool is there to help the users in finding the research not to decide what should happen with the user's plan. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Sustainable resource use}
The main objective of the tool is to help the user find existing research before they spent time and resources on their project. If the plans of the user should be changed due to previous research results it will prevent unnecessary duplication of experiments. This will save additional materials, energy consumption, equipment and computational resources. \newline \newline
However, similarities in research does not always mean that it is a waste. Sometimes replicating results is important for a study. In this case similarity is not a bad thing but help for new experiments. To ensure sustainable resource use, the tool needs to provide explanations containing the similarities. Then it is up to the user how they interpret the results in terms of relevancy.

\subsection*{Interpreting results and future work}
Our design aims to make results easy to interpret through a chatbot that provides sources and explains their relevance. Future work could help researchers identify ways to improve or differentiate their research. Previous work on the same topic does not necessarily mean that a researcher should abandon a project: there may still be useful differences to explore. In such cases, the tool could also help researchers consider better use of research resources.

\section*{F. User Narrative}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{F. User Narrative}
\noindent\textit{Word count: \Fwordcount / 500}\par\medskip

\noindent Dr. Alex sits at a desk inside the Eindhoven University of Technology chemistry lab, staring at a blank document. A new round of battery cathode tests is scheduled for next week, but Alex wants to make sure that this energy-heavy laboratory test won't just be a repeat of a study someone else has already done before. Hoping to find local equipment to share and existing data to build on, Alex opens a web browser and navigates to Eco-log.

Clicking the main button, "Explore My Research," Alex signs in instantly using his TU/e credentials via SURFconext. A clean workspace opens up, displaying a clear five-step progress tracker at the top.

Alex begins at Step 1: "Describe research". Alex types in the main research question about low-temperature cathode optimization. He adds the planned chemical methods. Then he selects TU/e as the home institution.

Moving to Step 2: "Clarify the proposal", a friendly AI consultation window pops up. It asks a quick question to clarify how the battery cells will be normalized. Alex types a short reply. The screen immediately organizes the plan into a neat checklist of three unique experiments (E1, E2 and E3).

Next comes Step 3: "Review related work". Instead of a messy list of links, the dashboard organizes matching academic papers by geographic distance. Alex filters the results to show projects from the same campus and nearby regional universities. Hence, getting a clear picture of the community of scientists and research projects in his area.

This data comes to life during Step 4: "Generate two trees". Alex explores the large, interactive visual layouts. Hovering over a branch on the Similarity \& Reuse Tree, a tooltip pops up showing exactly which past TU/e theses have used a matching experimental setup. Switching over to the Distinctiveness \& Research Gaps Tree, Alex clicks a node labeled "No close match found in reviewed sources". Hence, confirming that the new project will fill an unaddressed gap in the literature.

Finally, Alex reaches Step 5: "Explore recommendations". Under the "Same University" section, a card catches Alex's eye. A nearby research group right here at TU/e has an active profile listing a shared "tube furnace and coin-cell line plus a 2023 cycling dataset". A green sustainability badge labeled "Avoided measurement" explains that reusing this existing campus dataset may avoid repeating energy-heavy baseline synthesis tests. The tool also lists practical next steps, advising Alex to message the lab manager to check the furnace availability.

Satisfied with the plan, Alex clicks the green "Save Assessment" button at the bottom left to securely archive his session in a private history log. Closing his laptop, Alex stands up and walks down the hallway to find the local battery team. Thanks to Eco-log, Alex now knows exactly what existing work to build on. He also knows where the project's unique contribution lies and which nearby colleague can help him.
