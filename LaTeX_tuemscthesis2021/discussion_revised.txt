3 Discussion

3.1 Ethical Issues and Value Conflicts

The definition of sustainable research determines how activities are assessed. Hasselbalch and van Wynsberghe (2025) locate sustainability within sociotechnical relations of power, making the selection of criteria an ethical issue. If electricity consumption is prioritised, an intensive experiment may be rated unfavourably despite its scientific value. In the proposed assessment, environmental indicators would therefore be presented alongside explanations of research purpose. Comparisons would be restricted to activities with comparable objectives and quality requirements. Client agreement is needed on the weighting of criteria and the appropriateness of comparisons across disciplines. A single aggregate score could otherwise conceal disagreements about research priorities.

Numerical estimates may be treated as more authoritative than their evidence warrants. Lacoste et al. (2019) identify hardware, runtime and electricity supply as factors affecting emissions from machine learning training. Their method does not establish validity for every research activity. Apparent differences between cases may be smaller than calculation uncertainty. Measured and estimated values would therefore be distinguished, with missing information flagged and sources and boundaries disclosed. Input correction would remain available to researchers. These provisions support scrutiny, but cannot ensure that an assessment is critically examined.

The resource consumption of the assessment tool also requires justification. Lin (2023) argues for transparency about AI's energy use. Explicit calculations would be assigned to fixed formulas, while AI assistance would be limited to interpreting descriptions and drafting supported suggestions. Comparison with a structured non-AI assessment is needed to establish whether any additional benefit warrants the computation and review effort. Environmental improvement has not yet been demonstrated.

3.2 Design Barriers and Challenges

Defining a manageable assessment boundary is a practical constraint. Consumption from shared equipment and computing services may be difficult to attribute to individual activities. One representative workflow would be mapped with the client to establish which records are available. Estimates of equipment operation would state their exclusions. Replacement advice would require evidence about manufacturing impacts as well as operating savings.

Reference data and recommendations require maintenance. Sources, review dates and version histories would be recorded so that earlier assessments remain traceable. Following the institutional perspective of Hasselbalch and van Wynsberghe (2025), responsibility for correcting or withdrawing outdated guidance would need to be assigned at TU/e. The responsible role and available resources remain unresolved. For the proposed campus archive, outcome evidence and comparable activity conditions would require review before cases were reused. Higher assessment scores alone would not demonstrate reduced environmental impact. Reported outcomes would be distinguished from verified measurements, since selective reporting could favour successful cases and bias recommendations for future users of the proposed archive.

Evaluation would compare AI-assisted feedback with a structured checklist on the same cases. Evidence support, omitted issues and correction effort would be examined to establish whether further development is justified.
