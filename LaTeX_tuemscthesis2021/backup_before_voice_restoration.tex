\section{Introduction and Motivation}

\subsection{Topic, Motivation, Values and Needs}
Translating sustainability ambitions into research decisions requires information about specific activities and their resource use. In the ENSU client Q\&A, Borghuis described the electricity demand of a research computing setup, while Nickel discussed using the existing TU/e self-assessment to reflect on a research project (Appendix~\ref{app:process}). These observations motivate activity-level feedback; they do not establish how widespread the difficulty is among researchers.

The proposed tool would support researchers in examining selected environmental impacts before revising a plan. Sustainability must be considered alongside research quality: reducing consumption is not sufficient if an experiment can no longer answer its research question. Transparency about incomplete evidence and the ability to question recommendations are therefore central design needs.

\subsection{Objectives and Key Terms}
The design question is whether limited AI assistance can improve the usefulness of research self-assessment compared with a structured non-AI checklist using the same evidence and formulas. A large language model (LLM) would interpret activity descriptions and draft source-linked suggestions. Explicit calculations would be performed separately. Retrieval-augmented generation would supply relevant documents to the model, without guaranteeing that its interpretation is correct.

The initial output would present separate environmental indicators, evidence gaps and possible actions, with no aggregate sustainability or compliance score. This makes the disagreement between resource efficiency and research quality visible rather than resolving it through unvalidated weights. Benefits of AI assistance remain to be evaluated.

\subsection{Context and Scope}
The intended users are TU/e researchers, including students conducting research. The first worked case concerns battery research, covering selected laboratory operations and associated computing. Upstream material impacts would be included only where suitable supplier or lifecycle data were available; the result would not constitute a complete lifecycle assessment.

The tool would provide advice during planning and revision. It would neither approve research nor rank researchers across disciplines. This boundary follows Nickel's Q\&A comments that an additional institutional go/no-go procedure was not intended. A campus archive would support comparison of similar activities; its governance would require agreement before deployment.

\subsection{Tools, Methods and Frameworks}
Value Sensitive Design (VSD) provides the method for translating stakeholder concerns into requirements \citep{friedman2019shaping,vsdlab}. Conceptual investigation would identify affected groups and examine tensions such as resource reduction versus research quality. The resulting design choice is separate indicators rather than a single score (R2).

Empirical investigation begins with the client Q\&A. Researcher and supervisor walkthroughs would then test whether the proposed input fields and evidence displays fit actual work. Technical investigation would compare the checklist and AI-assisted versions, including correction effort and resource use (R6). The investigations would be revisited when feedback exposed a conflict between a feature and a stakeholder need.

The proposed expert interface would support reviewed source updates, with version histories and assigned maintenance responsibility (R4--R5). A local open-weight LLM, retrieval store and web interface remain candidate components. Local hosting would not by itself establish privacy or low resource use. Model and infrastructure selection would follow evaluation; policy updates would require review before activation.

\subsection{Design Strategy}
Development would follow evidence $\rightarrow$ requirement $\rightarrow$ prototype $\rightarrow$ evaluation $\rightarrow$ revision. Each requirement would retain its motivating source or stakeholder concern, the affected feature and a check, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:requirements}.

First, one battery-research activity would be mapped with the client to define required inputs and exclusions. A structured checklist with manual document lookup and fixed calculations would form the non-AI baseline. An AI-assisted version would use the same inputs, references and formulas; only description structuring, retrieval assistance and suggestion drafting would differ. This comparison would help isolate the contribution of AI rather than the benefit of better source material.

Researchers and a relevant subject expert would then examine the same cases, including complete records, missing electricity data and an unsuitable reference case. Evaluation would record unsupported claims, omitted issues, input corrections and review time. Resource use would be measured where feasible and otherwise reported as an estimate with its limits. A feature would be revised or removed if its output could not be traced to suitable evidence. Further development would require useful feedback without increased unsupported advice and with a defensible resource cost. No evaluation results or numerical success threshold are claimed at this stage.

\subsection{Anticipated Function and Purpose of the Tool}
A researcher would describe an activity and verify the extracted details before reviewing its assessment (Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}). Follow-up questions would address missing inputs; unresolved gaps would remain visible. The output would distinguish measured information from estimates and link recommendations to relevant evidence. Human review would concern whether a proposed change preserves the purpose and quality of the research. Responsibility for faulty calculations or outdated guidance would not be transferred entirely to the user.

For example, a battery researcher could describe material preparation, cell fabrication and performance testing (Figure~\ref{fig:activity}). Equipment runtime and electricity measurements could support selected operational estimates. Claims about material production would require appropriate upstream data. In their absence, the tool would identify the evidence needed rather than infer a complete footprint. Appendix~\ref{app:mockup} preserves the early group sketches alongside a revised interface concept showing evidence and actions without a score.

A proposed TU/e archive would store comparable research cases with activity conditions, model and advice versions, and follow-up evidence under pseudonymous identifiers. A fabrication change could be linked to subsequent material use and electricity measurements. AI could organise records and retrieve similar cases; a campus curator would approve evidence and guidance updates. Reuse would require consent and compatible objectives, boundaries and measurement methods. Unsuccessful or unchanged outcomes would also need recording to avoid favouring success stories. Archive roles and resources remain to be agreed with the client.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Ethical Issues and Value Conflicts}

The definition of sustainable research determines how activities are assessed. \citet{hasselbalch2025sustainability} locate sustainability within sociotechnical relations of power, making the selection of criteria an ethical issue. If electricity consumption is prioritised, an intensive experiment may be rated unfavourably despite its scientific value. In the proposed assessment, environmental indicators would therefore be presented alongside explanations of research purpose. Comparisons would be restricted to activities with comparable objectives and quality requirements. The initial design therefore omits aggregate scores and cross-disciplinary rankings. Client agreement is still needed on indicator selection and the conditions under which similar activities can be compared.

Numerical estimates may be treated as more authoritative than their evidence warrants. \citet{lacoste2019carbon} identify hardware, runtime and electricity supply as factors affecting emissions from machine learning training. Their method does not establish validity for every research activity. Apparent differences between cases may be smaller than calculation uncertainty. Measured and estimated values would therefore be distinguished, with missing information flagged and sources and boundaries disclosed. Input correction would remain available to researchers. These provisions support scrutiny, but cannot ensure that an assessment is critically examined.

The resource consumption of the assessment tool also requires justification. \citet{lin2023cost} argues for transparency about AI's energy use. Explicit calculations would be assigned to fixed formulas, while AI assistance would support descriptions and suggestions, including retrieval from the reviewed archive. Comparison with a structured non-AI assessment is needed to establish whether any additional benefit warrants the computation and review effort. Environmental improvement has not yet been demonstrated.

\subsection{Design Barriers and Challenges}

Defining a manageable assessment boundary is a practical constraint. Consumption from shared equipment and computing services may be difficult to attribute to individual activities. One representative workflow would be mapped with the client to establish which records are available. Estimates of equipment operation would state their exclusions. Replacement advice would require evidence about manufacturing impacts as well as operating savings.

Reference data and recommendations require maintenance. Sources, review dates and version histories would be recorded so that earlier assessments remain traceable. Following the institutional perspective of \citet{hasselbalch2025sustainability}, responsibility for correcting or withdrawing outdated guidance would need to be assigned at TU/e. The responsible role and available resources remain unresolved. For the proposed campus archive, outcome evidence and comparable activity conditions would require review before cases were reused. Higher assessment scores alone would not demonstrate reduced environmental impact. Reported outcomes would be distinguished from verified measurements, since selective reporting could favour successful cases and bias recommendations for future users of the proposed archive.

Evaluation would compare AI-assisted feedback with a structured checklist on the same cases. Evidence support, omitted issues and correction effort would be examined to establish whether further development is justified.
