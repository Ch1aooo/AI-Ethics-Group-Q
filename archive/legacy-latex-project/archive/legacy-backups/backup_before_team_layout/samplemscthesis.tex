\section{Introduction and Motivation}

\subsection{Topic, Motivation, Values and Needs}
According to TU/e’s Sustainability Visioning Report, TU/e has already seen sustainable development as a major part of campus development and trying to achieve the goal of fully embodying sustainability and proactively engaging with societal challenges in 2050. However, the researchers need to put these into practical activities; this refers to specific equipment and data, which is often confusing. This project is trying to explore whether AI can help in this progress while remaining transparent and supporting human judgement.

\subsection{Objectives and Key Terms}
In this project, AI support tool is proposed to help users judge and examine the potential environmental impacts, taking equipment use, runtime, electricity consumption and other relevant factors into account.

A large language model (LLM) is a language model with a large number of parameters, trained on extensive text data to support language understanding and generation. Here, LLM would provide supported suggestions based on the provided description of activities and use the formulas to calculate the environmental impacts through a separate module.

The design objective is trying to help the researchers get reliable self-reflection on their own project. The basic working flow contains: submit the plan, analysis, and assume the environmental effects by the LLM, compare it with the database we established, then score and give the final feedback with some practical advise and recommendations. The database will be based on the known data and results of the papers, and will also include some examples, aiming to be professional and reliable. Missing information should trigger questions or qualified feedback. Evaluation would examine evidence traceability, consistency and whether users find the feedback helpful when revising their plans.

\subsection{Context and Scope}

The project focuses on the Research domain at TU/e. Its intended users are researchers, including students conducting research, who want to reflect on the environmental impacts of their planned research activities. This tool is designed for the planning and revising the research activities, such as date processing and computational experiments. The assessment covers selected environmental impacts for which sufficient activity data and reference information are available.
The tool will not be used to manage campus facilities, evaluate university curricula, or decide which projects receive approval or funding. It will not provide a complete life-cycle assessment or rank researchers and projects across disciplines.

\subsection{Anticipated Function and Purpose of the Tool}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User and immediate goal:} TU/e researchers wanting to know the sustainability impact of their research.
    
    \item \textbf{Input:} A precise description of the research.
    
    \item \textbf{System actions:} The tool analyzes the research for potential sustainability impacts and, if needed, asks follow-up questions.
    
    \item \textbf{Output:} A guideline for researchers explaining how their research impacts sustainability.
    
    \item \textbf{Human review or correction:} Researchers need to carefully review the feedback from the tool.
    
    \item \textbf{Handling of missing information or uncertainty:} The tool continues asking questions when there is insufficient information.
\end{itemize} 

For this scenario, a Tu/e researcher describes their work to the tool. Their research is about developing a new battery for electric cars. 
Potential impacts can be, for example, the gathering and production of required materials for the battery. The production will of course require energy and potentially lots of costs. The tool will also look at the environmental impacts of the production.
The tool will ask questions about the materials used for the production of the new battery. With this information, the tool can then ask further questions about the costs, energy, etc. 
When the tool has all the necessary information, it can then provide a detailed guideline for the research containing the potential impact with explanations and possible improvements. 
It is now up to the researchers to review the guidelines from the tool and decide what they want to change. The AI is not responsible for these decisions, as it makes clear that the output is not an official approval for the research.


Figures~\ref{fig:workflow} and~\ref{fig:activity} present a design proposal for group confirmation, rather than implemented functionality. AI would structure descriptions and draft source-supported suggestions; a calculation module would apply explicit formulas. Missing inputs would be returned for clarification, with unsupported estimates withheld if the information remains unavailable.

A proposed TU/e sustainability case archive would retain activity conditions, the model and recommendation versions used, and follow-up evidence under a pseudonymous case identifier. For a teaching-material printing case, it could record print volume and settings, the advice adopted, and subsequent paper use alongside any assessment-score change. Comparable cases could then inform peer comparisons and revised suggestions. A higher score alone would not establish environmental improvement. Researchers would confirm outcomes and consent to reuse; a campus curator would review evidence and comparability before admitting cases or revising guidance. This extension to teaching activities would require client agreement on scope.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Ethical Issues and Value Conflicts}

The definition of sustainable research determines how activities are assessed. \citet{hasselbalch2025sustainability} locate sustainability within sociotechnical relations of power, making the selection of criteria an ethical issue. If electricity consumption is prioritised, an intensive experiment may be rated unfavourably despite its scientific value. In the proposed assessment, environmental indicators would therefore be presented alongside explanations of research purpose. Comparisons would be restricted to activities with comparable objectives and quality requirements. Client agreement is needed on the weighting of criteria and the appropriateness of comparisons across disciplines. A single aggregate score could otherwise conceal disagreements about research priorities.

Numerical estimates may be treated as more authoritative than their evidence warrants. \citet{lacoste2019carbon} identify hardware, runtime and electricity supply as factors affecting emissions from machine learning training. Their method does not establish validity for every research activity. Apparent differences between cases may be smaller than calculation uncertainty. Measured and estimated values would therefore be distinguished, with missing information flagged and sources and boundaries disclosed. Input correction would remain available to researchers. These provisions support scrutiny, but cannot ensure that an assessment is critically examined.

The resource consumption of the assessment tool also requires justification. \citet{lin2023cost} argues for transparency about AI's energy use. Explicit calculations would be assigned to fixed formulas, while AI assistance would be limited to interpreting descriptions and drafting supported suggestions. Comparison with a structured non-AI assessment is needed to establish whether any additional benefit warrants the computation and review effort. Environmental improvement has not yet been demonstrated.

\subsection{Design Barriers and Challenges}

Defining a manageable assessment boundary is a practical constraint. Consumption from shared equipment and computing services may be difficult to attribute to individual activities. One representative workflow would be mapped with the client to establish which records are available. Estimates of equipment operation would state their exclusions. Replacement advice would require evidence about manufacturing impacts as well as operating savings.

Reference data and recommendations require maintenance. Sources, review dates and version histories would be recorded so that earlier assessments remain traceable. Following the institutional perspective of \citet{hasselbalch2025sustainability}, responsibility for correcting or withdrawing outdated guidance would need to be assigned at TU/e. The responsible role and available resources remain unresolved. For the proposed campus archive, outcome evidence and comparable activity conditions would require review before cases were reused. Higher assessment scores alone would not demonstrate reduced environmental impact. Reported outcomes would be distinguished from verified measurements, since selective reporting could favour successful cases and bias recommendations for future users of the proposed archive.

Evaluation would compare AI-assisted feedback with a structured checklist on the same cases. Evidence support, omitted issues and correction effort would be examined to establish whether further development is justified.
